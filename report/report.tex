\begin{abstract}
\noindent We describe our system and the complete experimental record for the Sinhala MMLU Shared Task at ICTer 2026,
whose test set consists of 2,238 four-option multiple-choice questions from Sri Lankan 2025 school examinations
(Easy, Medium, Hard). Under the task's 8B-parameter, open-weight, no-retrieval rules and a time budget of a few days, we
use a single model, \texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it} (4.30B parameters), without fine-tuning, and study 55 inference-time
conditions: prompt wording, subject and role information, few-shot examples, option-order averaging, contextual
calibration, cloze scoring, 4-bit quantisation, a pretrained base model, chain-of-thought reasoning in Sinhala and in
English with different length limits, self-consistency, prompt ensembles, three Sinhala$\rightarrow$English
translation pipelines (NLLB-200, MADLAD-400, self-translation) with direct and reasoning-based answering, and prompts
that target negation and option-by-option judgement.
Conditions were screened on a 280-question stratified subset of the public (Easy-only) training data and the best were
confirmed on 1,483 held-out questions under selection rules fixed before the confirmation results were seen.
\textbf{Letting Gemma reason step by step in English about the Sinhala question, then scoring the answer digit, raises
accuracy from 38.2\% to 45.6\% (+7.4 points, Holm-corrected $p = 1.2\times10^{-6}$, $n=1{,}483$).} Translation
pipelines, calibration, option-order averaging, cloze scoring and self-consistency did not help. Translations drop
the negation of about 40\% of negated questions, which explains why translated input hurts most on exactly those
questions, whereas English reasoning over the original Sinhala handles them well. We also report a scoring pitfall
that silently reduces next-token evaluation of Gemma to chance: the space before the answer digit is a separate token,
so the reply must be pre-filled with ``\si{පිළිතුර}: '' (with a trailing space); without it only 5\% of the probability
falls on the four digits. Two submissions have been produced and validated: English reasoning with a teacher role,
limited to 512 and to 1,024 new tokens; their leaderboard scores are \todo{to be filled in after the Codabench upload}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
SinhalaMMLU \cite{pramodya2025} is the first multitask language-understanding benchmark for Sinhala, built from Sri
Lankan national school curricula and examinations, with questions grouped by difficulty: Easy (grades 6--8),
Medium (O/L level) and Hard (A/L level). The paper finds that open models up to 8B parameters are close to random
(25\%) and that even Qwen2.5-7B-chat reaches only 30.85\% on Easy questions zero-shot (Table~15 of the paper), while
Claude 3.5 Sonnet reaches 67.65\% overall. The ICTer 2026 shared task asks participants to improve on this under
strict constraints (Section~\ref{sec:task}).

Our constraints were mainly time and compute: work on the final system started on 24 October, three days before the
leaderboard closed, on a single rented RTX~3090. We therefore chose one strong small multilingual model and
investigated only inference-time methods, with a statistically careful protocol so that the many comparisons
would not mislead us.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible evaluation pipeline for Sinhala MCQs (\texttt{sinmmlu/}) with label-mass validity checks,
paired significance tests, and a strict separation of screening, confirmation and test data.
\item A systematic screen of 55 inference-time conditions for Gemma-3-4B-it on Sinhala exam questions, and
pre-registered confirmations of the best candidates on 1,483 held-out questions.
\item The finding that \emph{English} chain-of-thought over the untranslated Sinhala question gives a robust gain
of about +7 points, while machine translation (NLLB, MADLAD, self-translation) does not help, also not when combined
with reasoning, because translations frequently lose negation.
\item A documented scoring pitfall (answer pre-fill and the space token) and several other engineering errors
that we found and corrected, with their effect on results.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contamination check}
Every training question was compared with every test question by character 5-gram Jaccard similarity on a
normalised text (NFC, ZWJ/ZWNJ removed, punctuation and symbols mapped to spaces, lower-cased Latin, blank-runs
such as ``\ldots'' and ``\_\_\_\_'' removed), computed two ways and exactly for every candidate pair:
\emph{full} = question + options sorted alphabetically (years reorder options), and \emph{q} = question only (only for
questions of $\ge$ 25 characters). Classes: \textsc{remove} if full $\ge 0.5$; \textsc{review} if full is in
$[0.35, 0.5)$ or ($q \ge 0.5$ and full $\ge 0.2$); \textsc{ignored} if $q \ge 0.5$ and full $< 0.2$ (generic stems
such as ``choose the correctly spelled word''). Stricter thresholds (0.35/0.25) are defined for collected past papers.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Label scoring}
For a question $x$ with options $o_1,\dots,o_4$, a prompt $\pi(x)$ is rendered (with Gemma's chat template unless
stated otherwise), and the model's next-token distribution is read at the answer position. For each label
$\ell\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ we take $\log P(\ell \mid \pi(x))$ over the \emph{full} vocabulary, renormalise over the four labels,
and predict the argmax. This is the scoring used by the SinhalaMMLU paper for open models. We record the
\textbf{label mass} $\sum_\ell P(\ell\mid\pi(x))$ for every question: if it is low, the model wanted to say
something else, and the argmax over the four labels is weak evidence. A condition is only trusted if its mean label
mass is $\ge 0.5$. Prompts are never truncated (an over-long prompt raises an error), batches are left-padded with
explicit position ids, and any non-finite logit stops the run.

\subsection{The answer pre-fill and the space token}\label{sec:prefill}
The first test run of the pipeline on Gemma-3-4B-it showed a mean label mass of only about 0.05. Inspecting the
top next tokens revealed two causes. (i) Gemma begins its reply with a phrase such as
``\si{වැඩිම ගැළපෙන පිළිතුර}: 2 \ldots'' (``the most suitable answer: 2'') or with ``\texttt{**}'' (Markdown bold), so
the digit is not the first token. (ii) Gemma's tokenizer encodes ``\verb*| 2|'' as two tokens, a space and ``2'',
and has no single token for the digit with a leading space, so after ``\ldots:'' the model's next token is the space.
Table~\ref{tab:prefill} shows the effect on 40 questions.

All label-scored prompts are therefore scored with the reply pre-filled: in chat mode the model turn starts with
``\textit{answer-word}:\textvisiblespace'' (``\si{පිළිතුර}'' for Sinhala prompts, ``Answer'' for English ones), few-shot
example answers are written the same way, and raw prompts get a trailing space. Only the bare-digit token is scored.
With this, the label mass is $\ge 0.997$ for every valid condition. The zero label mass of all chat-off runs in
phase~0 (Table~\ref{tab:phase0}) is consistent with the same space-token effect. We did not test whether it affects
other published next-token evaluations of Sinhala.

\subsection{Option-order averaging and contextual calibration}
\textbf{Averaging} scores each question under the 4 cyclic option orders $\sigma_s(i) = (i+s) \bmod 4$, maps the four
renormalised label probabilities back to the original options, and averages them. \textbf{Contextual calibration}
\cite{zhao2021} estimates the model's label prior from three content-free inputs (``N/A'', the empty string, and
``[MASK]'', each used as the question and as all four options), averages their log-probabilities, and subtracts this
prior before renormalising. When the prompt shows the subject, one prior is estimated per subject.

\subsection{Cloze scoring}
The question is shown \emph{without} options, and each option text $o_i$ is scored as a continuation of the pre-filled
answer: $\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_t \log P(o_{i,t}\mid \pi(x), o_{i,<t})$ (mean log-probability per token). The argmax
over options is the prediction.

\subsection{Reasoning (chain of thought)}\label{sec:cot}
The model first generates a reasoning text with greedy decoding (at most 512 new tokens in English, 768 in Sinhala,
since Sinhala needs about three times as many tokens per word; the follow-up experiments and submission 2 use other
limits, Section~\ref{sec:followup}). We then (i) find the last ``Answer: $d$'' or
``\si{පිළිතුර}: $d$'' it wrote (the \emph{parse rate} is the fraction of questions where one is found), (ii) cut the
reasoning just before it, (iii) append the cue ``\textbackslash n\textbackslash n Answer:\textvisiblespace'' (or
``\si{පිළිතුර}:\textvisiblespace''), and (iv) score the four labels at that position as above. Every question therefore
gets a probability vector, also when the reasoning was cut off by the length limit; where an answer was stated,
the scored answer agrees with it in 99.6--100\% of cases. Generation is deterministic (greedy, fixed seed 42), and the
reasoning texts are cached so that an interrupted run resumes.

\subsection{Few-shot examples}
$k$-shot prompts ($k\in\{1,3,5\}$) put $k$ solved examples \emph{of the same subject} before the question, as earlier
chat turns (user: the example question; assistant: ``\si{පිළිතුර}: $d$''). The examples come only from the fixed
\texttt{fewshot\_pool}, in a fixed order, so every question of a subject sees the same examples; nothing is chosen by
similarity to the question. Test subjects are matched to training subjects by the first three letters of the
English subject name (``Art''$\leftrightarrow$``Arts'', ``Health''$\leftrightarrow$``Health and Physical Science'',
``Sinhala''$\leftrightarrow$``Sinhala language and literature''); subjects without training examples (most Medium and
Hard subjects) use a fixed generic set (the first example of each subject).

\paragraph{Worked examples for reasoning.} For \cond{cot\_en\_fewshot2}, Gemma generated English reasoning (prompt
\cond{cot\_en}) for the 70 pool questions; the 28 whose stated answer was correct were kept, up to and including
their ``Answer: $d$'' line, as two worked examples per subject. Buddhism had no correct rationale and uses the generic
fallback. These rationales are model-written, from public training data only.

\subsection{Translation pipelines}\label{sec:translate}
The question and each option are translated separately into English and cached:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{NLLB}: \texttt{facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M} \cite{nllb2022} (revision \texttt{f8d333a0\ldots},
615M parameters, CC-BY-NC~4.0), source \texttt{sin\_Sinh}, target \texttt{eng\_Latn}.
\item \textbf{MADLAD}: \texttt{google/madlad400-3b-mt} \cite{madlad2023} (revision \texttt{fa184c67\ldots}, 2.94B
parameters, Apache~2.0), input prefix ``\texttt{<2en>}''.
\item \textbf{Self}: Gemma-3-4B-it itself, prompted ``Translate the following Sinhala text into English. Write only the
English translation, nothing else.'' (greedy, $\le$ 512 new tokens).
\end{itemize}
NLLB and MADLAD are sentence-level systems: texts are split into lines and sentences (after ``.'', ``?'', ``!''),
each piece is translated with beam search (4 beams, $\le$ 256 new tokens), and the pieces are re-joined;
pieces without Sinhala letters are copied unchanged. Gemma then answers one of three prompt variants: English only,
bilingual (Sinhala original followed by the English translation, with a note that the translation may contain errors),
or the Sinhala question with English options. On the subset, NLLB translated 1,402 unique sentence pieces in about a
minute; MADLAD translated 1,437 pieces (several minutes, run time not recorded).

\subsection{Ensembles}
An ensemble averages the four option probabilities of finished conditions of the \emph{same} model (so the parameter
count does not grow) and takes the argmax.

\subsection{Prompts}
All prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. The baseline \cond{paper\_nosubject} is Appendix~B of the paper
without the subject line.

\section{Experimental protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Stages.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Smoke test}: 8 questions through every code path (no results recorded). It found the pre-fill problem.
\item \textbf{Screening} on \texttt{inference\_subset} ($n$=280) in three waves: (1) prompt wording, chat template,
averaging, calibration, cloze, 4-bit, model references; (2) few-shot, NLLB and self-translation; (3) reasoning in
Sinhala and English, MADLAD.
\item \textbf{Combinations} of the screening winners (also on the subset).
\item \textbf{Confirmation} on \texttt{inference\_rest} ($n$=1,483) under a rule written into our lab notebook
before the combination results were seen: take the four best \emph{valid} conditions on the subset (label mass
$\ge$ 0.5, parameter budget respected, ensembles only within one model) plus the baseline; a condition is
\emph{adopted} if it beats the baseline by $\ge$ 1 point with Holm-corrected exact McNemar $p<0.05$ over the
confirmed comparisons; the most accurate adopted condition wins; if none is adopted, the baseline is submitted.
\item \textbf{Test}: the winner is run once on the 2,238 test questions.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Statistics.} Accuracy with a 95\% Wilson interval; for every condition against the baseline on the same
questions: the exact two-sided McNemar test on the discordant pairs, Holm--Bonferroni correction over all conditions
compared in that table, and a paired bootstrap 95\% interval of the accuracy gain (10,000 resamples of questions,
seed 0).

\paragraph{Hardware and software.} RunPod pod with one NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3090 (24~GB), 128 vCPUs, 251~GB RAM;
Python 3.12.3, PyTorch 2.8.0 (CUDA 12.8), Transformers 5.18.0, Accelerate 1.15.0, bitsandbytes 0.50.2, PEFT 0.21.1.
Gemma in bfloat16 with batch size 16 for scoring and generation; batches are split in halves automatically on GPU
out-of-memory. 4-bit runs use NF4 with bfloat16 compute.

\paragraph{Observations on the screening set.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reasoning in English is the strongest single change} (+10.4, the only single condition significant after
Holm). Reasoning in Sinhala helps less (+5.7) and is slower (450\,s against 350\,s for 280 questions).
\item \textbf{Telling the model the subject} helps (+3 to +6), as the paper found; a Sinhala teacher role helps most.
English instructions alone help about as much (+4.3).
\item \textbf{Few-shot} examples help modestly (+3 to +5); 3 examples are as good as 5.
\item \textbf{Option-order averaging} changes little (+1.1): Gemma has no strong position preference after the
pre-fill fix. \textbf{Calibration hurts} ($-$3.2): it shifts predictions towards option 2 (46.8\% of predictions,
against 26.4\% of gold answers), i.e.\ the content-free prior is a poor estimate of the model's bias here.
\item \textbf{Cloze scoring is worse} ($-$6.1): Sinhala options of very different lengths and forms are not
comparable by mean token log-probability.
\item \textbf{4-bit NF4 costs nothing measurable} ($-$0.7, $p$=0.90), relevant if the organisers reproduce on a
smaller GPU.
\item \textbf{The pretrained base model} scores surprisingly well (45.0\%) but has a strong preference for option 1
(46.4\% of its predictions; 57.5\% with the subject prompt) and a lower label mass (0.92), and it cannot follow the
chat-style reasoning prompts that won.
\item \textbf{Letters A--D} are not usable: Gemma answers with a Sinhala letter (e.g.\ \si{ඊ}) or a digit, so only 0.2\%
of the probability falls on the Latin letters.
\item \textbf{NLLB and self-translation do not help} (between $-$2.5 and +2.9). MADLAD translation helps more (+6.4
English only, +5.4 bilingual) but stays below English reasoning; Gemma + MADLAD (7.24B) would be within the
parameter limit.
\item \textbf{Gemma-3-1B-it is at chance} (25.7\%).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison with the paper}
Our Easy-level numbers are on public-train questions (2018--2023) and the paper's on its own Easy split, so the
comparison is indicative only. The paper reports zero-shot Easy accuracy of 30.85\% for Qwen2.5-7B-chat, 34.31\% for
Llama-3.1-8B and 45.11\% for Qwen2.5-72B-chat (Table~15 of \cite{pramodya2025}). Gemma-3-4B-it scored 38.2\% with
the paper's own prompt (pre-filled), and 45.6\% with English reasoning, i.e.\ at the level the paper reports for a
72B model.

\subsection{Test predictions: submission 1}
\cond{cot\_en\_role} was run on all 2,238 test questions in 3,083\,s on the RTX~3090 (longest input 961 tokens).
The file passed format validation twice (on the pod and locally): 2,238 lines, keys identical to the test set, every
answer in \{``1'',``2'',``3'',``4''\}. The model stated an answer in only 62.4\% of its reasoning texts (76.6\% on the
confirmation set), probably because the longer Medium and Hard questions more often reach the 512-token limit; every
question still receives a scored answer, and the mean label mass after the cue was 0.998. This motivated the
follow-up experiments below.

\section{Final system}\label{sec:system}
The current final system is submission 2; submission 1 differs only in the reasoning limit (512 tokens).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model}: \texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it}, revision \texttt{093f9f38\ldots}, bfloat16, 4,300,079,472
parameters (only component; 8B limit respected).
\item \textbf{Input}: NFC normalisation, ZWNJ removed, ZWJ kept; options numbered ``1.''--``4.''; the English subject
name from the test file.
\item \textbf{Prompt} (user turn of Gemma's chat template):
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small,frame=single]
You are an expert Sri Lankan teacher of {subject}.
The following is a multiple choice question in Sinhala about {subject}.
Think step by step in English, then choose the correct or most suitable answer from the options 1, 2, 3, 4.
End your reply with "Answer: <number>".

Question: {Sinhala question}
1. {option 1}
...
4. {option 4}
\end{Verbatim}
\item \textbf{Decoding}: greedy, at most 1,024 new tokens (submission 2; 512 in submission 1); the stated answer (if
any) is cut, ``\textbackslash n\textbackslash n Answer:\textvisiblespace'' appended, and the argmax over the digit
tokens 1--4 is the prediction.
\item \textbf{No} retrieval, no fine-tuning, no external data, no closed API, no test-time adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
\paragraph{Code.} Package \texttt{sinmmlu/}: \texttt{data} (loading, normalisation, ids, permutations), \texttt{prompts}
(prompt registry), \texttt{scoring} (averaging, calibration), \texttt{model} (loading, label/cloze scoring, generation),
\texttt{evaluate} (runs conditions), \texttt{translate}, \texttt{ensemble}, \texttt{rationales}, \texttt{report}
(paired statistics), \texttt{select\_winner}, \texttt{predict\_winner}, \texttt{submit} (format validation),
\texttt{build\_dev}, \texttt{dedup}, \texttt{tokens}, \texttt{debug}, and the analysis-only \texttt{negation} and
\texttt{qtypes}. 36 offline tests (including a fake bigram model that checks batched label and cloze scoring against
an unbatched reference, a simulated out-of-memory split, and a stub model for self-consistency) run without a GPU.
The code is in git (branch \texttt{initial-setup}, commit \texttt{4a4aa89}). Every experiment stores its configuration, model revision, parameter count, library versions, GPU and
git revision in \texttt{outputs/<experiment>/config.json}.

\paragraph{Commands} (RunPod, after uploading the code and data):
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small,frame=single]
export HF_TOKEN=...                      # read token; Gemma licence accepted
bash runpod/setup.sh                     # GPU check, pinned libraries, dev files, tests, model downloads
bash runpod/run_subset.sh smoke          # then wave1, wave2, wave3, combos, confirm
bash runpod/select_and_test.sh           # selection rule -> submission 1
bash runpod/run_subset.sh cheap          # then translation, qtypes (follow-up screening)
bash runpod/confirm2.sh                  # second confirmation + selection -> submission 2
# or directly, the final system only:
python -m sinmmlu.evaluate --model configs/models/gemma3_4b_it.yaml --data test \
       --prompt cot_en_role --chat on --max-new-tokens 1024
\end{Verbatim}
The submission is written to
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
outputs/gemma3_4b_it__none__cot_en_role__chaton__p1__c0__trnone__mt1024__test/predictions.zip
\end{Verbatim}
Greedy decoding makes the run deterministic up to floating-point differences between GPUs.

\paragraph{Cost.} All runs together took about 10 GPU-hours on one RTX~3090: a label-scored condition takes 5--42\,s
for 280 questions (few-shot prompts are the slowest), a reasoning condition 6--9 minutes for 280 questions and 31--47
minutes for 1,483, and a test run 51 (512 tokens) to 71 minutes (1,024 tokens).

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{All development data is Easy.} We have no labelled Medium or Hard questions, so the gains may not transfer
to O/L and A/L questions, which are two thirds of the metric. In particular we cannot measure whether the 140 answers
that changed between submissions 1 and 2 (cut-off reasoning completed) are improvements; only the leaderboard shows it.
\item \textbf{Single model, no training.} Fine-tuning (for example on Global-MMLU-si \cite{globalmmlu2025} and the
public training data) was out of time; the paper rates the naturalness of Global-MMLU-si questions at 71/100
against 97/100 for native questions, so such data would need care.
\item \textbf{Screening noise.} With 280 questions, gains below about 7 points cannot be separated; two subset results
did not replicate. Only the confirmation results should be quoted as findings.
\item \textbf{Parameter-count error} at selection time (corrected; no effect on the decision).
\item \textbf{Process notes.} When submission 1 was checked, the counts of predicted answers (1--4) on the test set
were printed once; for submission 2, the number of answers that differ from submission 1 was counted. Neither uses
labels, nothing was chosen from them, and no other test-set statistic was computed.
\item \textbf{Lost raw outputs.} The rented pod was terminated on 25 October, which deleted its volume before the raw
outputs of the follow-up experiments (Section~\ref{sec:followup}) had been copied. All numbers in this report were
recorded in our lab notebook before that; the per-question predictions and reasoning texts of those runs, some
per-type cells (Table~\ref{tab:qtypes}) and the translation caches would have to be regenerated (greedy decoding and
fixed seeds make this possible). Raw outputs of the screening waves, combinations, the first confirmation and the
submission-1 test run, and both submission files, are kept.
\item \textbf{Licences.} NLLB (used only in screening) is CC-BY-NC~4.0; the final system uses only Gemma (Gemma
terms of use).
\end{itemize}

\section{Next steps}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compare the leaderboard scores of submissions 1 and 2 (Medium/Hard effect of the longer reasoning limit).
\item Fine-tuning with LoRA on Global-MMLU-si (and the public training data, if the team agrees), judged by the same
confirmation protocol, with \textbf{negation-focused training data} (for example ``which is NOT \ldots'' questions
and negated/non-negated question pairs) and a separate negation evaluation, since negation is the clearest failure
mode of direct scoring and of translated input (Section~\ref{sec:negation}).
\item The three-prompt reasoning ensemble (+1.1 on the confirmation set, not significant) if test-time compute allows.
\end{enumerate}
Explored and not pursued: self-consistency, translation pipelines with and without reasoning, option-by-option
judgement, calibration, cloze scoring.
